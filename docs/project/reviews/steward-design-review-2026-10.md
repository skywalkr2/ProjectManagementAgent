# Second opinion: daily steward design

This review treats `docs/design/06-engine.md` and the current Phase S roadmap as the intended design. The proposal and scenarios are useful context, but several statements in the proposal appear to be older than decisions recorded in the roadmap.

## 1. Where a non-technical person may lose trust in the first week

- **“Promises from everything” sounds broader than the first run.** The design promises commitments from incoming and sent mail, notes, and other sources. The first result, however, reads two weeks of mail and only includes sent mail when the person has added a sent-folder source; the design says that folder is not yet offered automatically by sign-in. A person can see a credible first result while believing Reginald checked more of their history than he did. The coverage line and “Missed one” are good safeguards, but the first screen should say which folders and dates were actually included, and make the sent-folder gap prominent. (`06-engine.md` §6.32, lines 2901–2939; `roadmap-to-1.0.md` lines 720–724.)
- **The first useful result asks for trust early.** The walkthrough connects a mailbox and an AI account before the person sees what promises were found, though it does show the look-back range and estimate before Start. The five-minute payoff is fragile if sign-in, mailbox history limits, or a missing sent folder delays the result. Before Start, say which folders and dates will be read, what the AI account receives, and what will be missing. (`06-engine.md` §6.32, lines 2929–2939; `steward-proposal.md` lines 27–30 and 104–115.)
- **One “Personal” project is a useful default, but may sound like a work project.** The later design makes this a normal project with areas and projects under it. A person who does not think in projects may not know where a bill, a medical appointment, or a family commitment belongs. Starting flat and proposing a folder only after a pattern appears helps; the first screen should explain that this is simply the place for personal items and that filing can wait. (`06-engine.md` §6.32, lines 2827–2844; `roadmap-to-1.0.md` lines 824–830.)
- **The privacy boundary is strong but takes work to understand.** The design combines one Today across areas with different data levels and allowed AI accounts. It also says what leaves the computer should be visible and that a draft must not draw on another area's knowledge. A person can still wonder which account will read a newly filed personal message, especially when a suggestion moves it between areas. Show the area and account on the item before it is used, and make a move's privacy effect part of the confirmation. (`roadmap-to-1.0.md` lines 824–830; `06-engine.md` §6.32, lines 2841–2844.)

## 2. Ways a promise, reminder, or brief could be wrong

- **A date may be confidently wrong even when the promise is real.** The design keeps the exact due words, separates “said” from “inferred,” displays “Check this date” when the date rules are uncertain, and leaves conditions such as “once the keys arrive” undated. Those controls make the reasoning inspectable. They do not make an inferred date correct; the design should also state whether a date marked for checking can trigger an “at risk” line or reminder before the person resolves it. (`06-engine.md` §6.33, lines 3036–3072 and 3184–3205.)
- **The design is much safer about endings than about first detection.** An ending from incoming text is held for the person with its evidence and two clear choices; a follow-up is explicitly not completion. The roadmap also measures wrong endings and silent closes. New promises are shown with their source words and a one-click correction. Because data changes, including actions, apply after checks while endings wait for a Keep, say plainly on the first promise list whether each item is already in To-dos or is waiting for a decision. (`06-engine.md` §6.7, lines 501–505; §6.33, lines 3153–3169 and 3197–3205; `roadmap-to-1.0.md` lines 728–735 and 841–843.)
- **A second “non-answer” can bring a chase back, but the message may still be an answer in context.** The design permits one non-answer to quiet the chase clock and makes a second one bring the line back. This catches empty reassurances, but it assumes the assistant can distinguish “I don't know yet” from a substantive answer, and that another nudge is welcome. The evidence and the person's choices help; make “not a reply needed” or “wait until…” available on the line, and ensure the person's correction changes only that item. (`06-engine.md` §6.33, lines 3159–3182; `steward-scenarios.md` lines 17–24 and 56–59.)
- **A late reminder may say the computer was asleep when it was not.** The reminder line is shown on waking and never dropped, and quiet-hours reminders are grouped. But the detailed design says lateness is inferred from how long the reminder went unshown because the engine does not know whether the computer slept. “While this computer was asleep” can therefore misstate what happened if the app was closed or notifications were unavailable. Call it “missed while Thimbleby was closed” only if that can be known; otherwise use “overdue” and show the due time. A calendar alarm or reminder email can reach someone while the computer sleeps, but those routes require a separate approved write. (`06-engine.md` §6.32, lines 3013–3032; §6.32, lines 2820–2824.)
- **A quiet brief can look like an all-clear when a source is stale.** The brief's rules require one plain fact per line, omit empty sections, and call a quiet day one line. The coverage design distinguishes “couldn't read” from “read, nothing new,” which helps. Keep that freshness information attached to the brief's all-clear; otherwise a mailbox that stopped updating can look like a calm day. (`06-engine.md` §6.32, lines 2796–2804 and 2940–2944.)
- **A capacity warning can overstate what the calendar knows.** The design checks free calendar time against estimates, preserves fixed tasks, and proposes moves without scheduling automatically. It still assumes the connected calendars contain the person's real obligations and that the estimates are useful. A parent may have school pickup or care work that is not on a calendar; a freelancer may reserve no time for admin. Label the result as a calendar-based estimate, show which calendars and work hours were counted, and let the person protect time that is not an event. (`roadmap-to-1.0.md` lines 791–801; `steward-scenarios.md` lines 52–55.)

## 3. Assumptions that may not hold

- **Mail is not the whole stream of commitments.** The first gate is deliberately limited to the owner's personal mail, and the first result reads a bounded mail window. Voice notes, forwarded mail, a watched folder, and a phone capture box are later work. A freelancer may make commitments in calls, chat, or a client portal; a parent may use a family calendar another person manages. Until those sources are connected, the interface should avoid implying that it remembers every promise. (`roadmap-to-1.0.md` lines 712–726 and 831–832; `steward-proposal.md` lines 59–62.)
- **Calendar time is treated as capacity.** This is an understandable first approximation, but it will be weak for people whose calendars contain only meetings or who have irregular care, travel, and admin time. The optional estimates and “no automatic scheduling” reduce the harm; the capacity result should remain an invitation to review, never a verdict about what can fit. (`roadmap-to-1.0.md` lines 791–801.)
- **The design assumes one person can make sense of shared obligations.** It covers multiple calendars and distinguishes who owes whom, but family and small-business commitments can belong to a household, an assistant, or a team. The proposal defers shared household roles until organization roles exist. Make ownership and calendar organizer visible so the assistant does not treat access to a shared calendar as authority to change someone else's event. (`steward-proposal.md` lines 43–52 and 83–85; `roadmap-to-1.0.md` lines 768–775.)
- **The day is assumed to have a predictable review rhythm.** A morning brief, evening wrap-up, Monday briefing, quiet hours, and a daily suggestion cap suit many people, but freelancers and parents may start and stop at uneven times. The settings and missed-while-away line help. Keep the brief available on demand and avoid making an unread morning page itself feel like a missed task. (`06-engine.md` §6.32, lines 2796–2804 and 2815–2819; `roadmap-to-1.0.md` lines 802–807.)

## 4. Contradictions and stale statements

- **The proposal puts Phase S after installers; the roadmap puts it before them.** The proposal's order says promises and people-memory work starts after phase I, while the roadmap's current heading says Phase S follows B and precedes I. The roadmap also says the owner reordered the work to make dependable promise memory the reason to choose the product. Update the proposal's old schedule so it does not send contributors to the wrong phase. (`steward-proposal.md` lines 97–100; `roadmap-to-1.0.md` lines 698–706.)
- **The proposal places the Monday briefing in Phase B; the roadmap moves it to S2.** The roadmap now treats it as the weekly form of the morning brief in Phase S2. This is a small but visible source of planning confusion. (`steward-proposal.md` lines 38–42; `roadmap-to-1.0.md` lines 779–790.)
- **“Reminders on any dated thing” is broader than the detailed reminder design.** The proposal and 6.32 include dated facts and calendar entries. The built reminder behavior described later is attached to an open action with `remind_at`. The design should say whether facts and calendar events can carry reminders directly or whether they must first become actions. (`steward-proposal.md` lines 31–34; `06-engine.md` §6.32, lines 2796–2798 and 3013–3032.)
- **Approved writes are both a decided 1.0 feature and an open question in the proposal.** The roadmap records approved mail and calendar management as decided, and 6.32 describes those acts after approval. The proposal still asks whether mail and calendar should remain read-only. Mark that question as resolved or point to the decision. (`steward-proposal.md` lines 88–100 and 117–126; `06-engine.md` §6.32, lines 2820–2824; `roadmap-to-1.0.md` lines 750–775 and 845–848.)

## 5. Three changes I would make first

1. **Define the first-run coverage contract.** Before a person connects mail, state exactly which account, folders, dates, and message types will be read; show which were actually returned; explain whether sent mail is included; and make the limits visible on the first promise list. The product's central claim is memory across commitments, so an incomplete first result must not look complete. (`06-engine.md` §6.32, lines 2901–2939.)
2. **Make uncertainty and provenance unavoidable on every promise line.** Keep the exact source words, who owes whom, whether it was said or inferred, and any uncertain date together. Require the person to resolve an uncertain date before treating it as a firm reminder, deadline, or “at risk” fact. Keep every ending held until the person decides. The design already has most of these pieces; make their presentation and the review gate explicit in the roadmap tests. (`06-engine.md` §6.33, lines 3042–3072 and 3153–3169; `roadmap-to-1.0.md` lines 728–735.)
3. **Settle reminder coverage and sleep wording before the brief depends on it.** Decide which dated facts and calendar events can be reminded directly, how a sleeping or closed computer is detected, and which approved route reaches the person. Use “overdue” unless sleep is known. The brief should say what was and was not read before it gives a quiet-day message. (`06-engine.md` §6.32, lines 2796–2804 and 3013–3032.)

## Uncertainties

I read the detailed reminder behavior as narrower than the overview because it currently names open actions as the objects that fire. If dated facts or calendar events are converted into actions elsewhere, that should be stated at the point where the reminder is created. This review checks the design and roadmap text; it does not claim that each planned behavior is already implemented or tested in the code.
