Practice for reading notes, minutes and messages (core playbook):

- Pull out every action, one per task. An action is a verb that does something: "Send the final file to the printer",
  never "Confirm the file" or "Check whether the file is ready". Name every owner the input names; a shared action lists
  each person. If the input doesn't say who, leave owners empty; the user will pick.
- Word each action so it can stand on its own, read by someone who has not seen the input: name what it is done to and
  for whom, never "send it", "follow up on that" or "do the above". An action that points at something unseen is
  returned to be reworded, and one for someone else that still does is held for the person.
- When the input says an open action is done, close it with the words that say so, rather than adding a note.
- A fact is something the input states as true (a date, a decision, a number). A note is anything else worth keeping.
- Before filing a question, check the open questions: if the input answers one, record the answer instead.
- Write each question for its reader: say only what they need to answer, ask the whole gap in one question, and link
  what it is about with a link they can open. Decisions for the user are questions to the user, except a choice someone asks
  the user directly, which is a reply the user owes.
- A drafted reply names where its answer comes from in words, never an id the reader can't open.
- Don't ask people for things the input or the records already say.
- Keep wording plain: one item per line, no lists crammed into one sentence.
- A promise is something someone owes someone, and it is an action. Read it both ways:
  - The user's own "I'll ..." (in their sent mail or their notes) is the user's to-do, promised to whoever they wrote to.
  - Someone else's "I'll get back to you by ..." is that person's action, promised to the user: the user is waiting on it.
  - A promise made on someone's behalf ("I told the client she would do it") is not theirs until they accept it.
  - A promise between two other people, seen in a forward, stays between them: keep who said it and who it was said to.
  - It is owed to whoever it is said to: with the user only in Cc, to the people it is addressed to; a named third party
    ("I'll send Sam the plan") is owed it; a team's "we'll" for no one named is not owed to the user.
- Take the date from the words, as written, including a leading "on", "by" or "due" and a day of the month ("due Oct
  22", "by the 25th"). "Early next week" or "toward the end of the month" is not a day; "once the keys arrive" or "after
  the workshop" is a condition: with no day named, its words are the due, and the promise has no date until the
  condition is met. Never put a date on a condition, and never leave its words out. A promise with both ("Friday, if
  the committee approves") keeps the day as its due and the condition beside it, and its text says the condition.
- Count only what is firmly promised. These are not promises:
  - quoted history, a signature or slogan, a forward, a template and example wording, which are never new;
  - a hope, a maybe, a suggestion ("we should"), a question ("could I send it Friday instead?"), an offer ("I can send
    it if you need it") or a refusal ("I can't promise an answer");
  - sarcasm and jokes, a request nobody accepted, and a promise to hold something back.
- Take a promise for exactly what it says:
  - "I'll try" or "I'll take a look" promises the attempt and the report, not the result.
  - A promise with a condition waits on hold for it ("once the keys arrive"); a date tied to a meeting follows the
    meeting.
  - A promise is one person's word: two who each owe their own part are two promises. Work two people do together is
    one action with both as owners and no one it is owed to.
    "We'll send it" with no individual named leaves the owners empty so the user picks one.
  - Two promises in one message are two actions, each with its own date.
- A promise ends only when the input says so:
  - "As promised, I've attached it" or "It's back on your shelf" closes the matching open action; it never adds one.
  - A new date given and accepted moves the action; a date only proposed ("could we do Thursday instead?") moves
    nothing. A correction to an open promise's date moves that promise; it never adds a second one.
  - A cancellation ("we no longer need it") drops the action.
  - Done needs delivery: close an action only when the words say it was delivered ("she sent it", "it's in the
    deck"). Words about learning it elsewhere, moving it to a meeting or it no longer mattering ("moot", "covered by
    the planning sessions", "drop it") drop it, with the person's words as the reason and nobody credited.
  - "Closed" isn't done: "mark it closed" with no delivery words is neither, and the person is asked whether it was
    done by its owner or is no longer needed.
  - A partial delivery keeps what is left open and credits what arrived.
  - A reply that doesn't answer is news on the open action, not an ending, and the waiting continues. It may
    acknowledge ("thanks for checking in"), praise ("the layout looks lovely"), defer ("I'll get back to you"), share
    enthusiasm, turn to another subject or answer a different question. Never skip it as nothing to record, and never
    read it as the work done, a call-off or a new date.
  - A follow-up or a nudge is never completion.
  - Someone saying they can't make a date is news on the matching promise, not a new date.
